# Isomorphism #2: Northern Chan Sky-Gazing and Dzogchen Tögal Sky-Gazing

## A Structural Comparison of T2834 *Dasheng wu fangbian* 大乘五方便 and *Thod rgal* Practice

**Date**: 2026-09-13  
**Status**: Original comparative analysis — no prior academic study identified  
**Primary source**: T2834 大乘無生方便門 (Dunhuang recension, Northern Chan)  
**Comparative referent**: Tögal (*thod rgal*) sky-gazing as attested in Nyingmapa instructional literature

---

## SECTION A: Side-by-Side Structural Comparison

### 1. The T2834 Sky-Gazing Sequence: Reconstruction

The T2834 passage embeds a complete sky-gazing meditation instruction within a catechetical dialogue between master (*heshang* 和尚) and student (*zi* 子). The full operational sequence, reconstructed from the text, proceeds as follows:

**Step 1 — Doctrinal ground** (引經):
> 金剛經云：凡所有相皆是虛妄。  
> "The *Diamond Sūtra* says: all that has characteristics is illusory."

The practice is framed within Prajñāpāramitā emptiness doctrine. This doctrinal anchoring has no parallel in tögal, which grounds itself in the *gzhi* (ground) of Dzogchen ontology.

**Step 2 — Prerequisite: mind already pure** (前提):
> 看心若淨名淨心地。  
> "When gazing at mind, if [it is] pure, [this is] called the ground of pure mind."

The sky-gazing practice is introduced *after* the mind has already been established in purity through prior *kàn xīn* 看心 practice. This is a **second-stage instruction**, not a standalone technique. [Textual evidence, not speculation.]

**Step 3 — Somatic relaxation** (身法):
> 莫卷縮身心舒展身心。  
> "Do not contract body-mind; relax and extend body-mind."

**Step 4 — Far gazing into open space** (遠看):
> 放曠遠看平等盡虛空看。  
> "Release [attention] widely, gaze far, gaze equally throughout the entirety of space."

**Step 5 — Dialogical verification, first round** (驗證一):
> 和問言：見何物？  
> 子云：一物不見。  
> "Master asks: 'What do you see?' Student says: 'Not a single thing.'"

**Step 6 — Refinement: subtle gazing at purity** (細看):
> 和：看淨細細看。即用淨心眼無邊無涯除遠看。  
> "Master: 'Gaze at purity, subtly, subtly gaze. Use the pure mind-eye, boundless and limitless, gaze far, removing [obstructions].'"

The term 淨心眼 *jìng xīn yǎn* ("pure mind-eye") is remarkable. It posits a mode of seeing that is neither the physical eye nor ordinary mental imagery but a purified perceptual faculty. This concept has structural affinity with the Dzogchen notion of *rig pa'i mig* ("wisdom-eye" or "eye of awareness").

**Step 7 — Unobstructed gazing** (無障礙看):
> 和言：問無障礙看。和問：見何物？  
> 答：一物不見。  
> "'Gaze without obstruction.' 'What do you see?' 'Not a single thing.'"

**Step 8 — Omnidirectional simultaneous gazing** (全方位看):
> 向前遠看。向後遠看。四維上下一時平等看。盡虛空看。  
> "Gaze far forward. Gaze far behind. All four directions, above and below, simultaneously gaze equally. Gaze to the ends of space."

**Step 9 — Continuity instruction** (不間斷):
> 長用淨心眼看。莫間斷亦不限多少看。使得者然身心調用無障礙。  
> "Continuously use the pure mind-eye to gaze. Do not interrupt; do not limit [sessions] to any quantity. This enables body-mind to be harmonized and used without obstruction."

**Step 10 — Result: purification of all six sense faculties** (果):
> 如是長時無斷用虛空無一物。清淨無有相。常令不間斷。從此永離障。  
> 眼根清淨。眼根離障。耳根清淨。耳離根障。如是乃至六根清淨。六根離障。一切無礙是即解脫。  
> "Thus, over long periods without interruption, using space [in which there is] not a single thing, pure, without characteristics, constantly without interruption — from this, permanently leave behind obstructions. The eye-faculty is purified... the ear-faculty is purified... thus up to all six sense faculties purified. All six faculties free of obstruction. Complete non-obstruction — this is liberation."

### 2. Five Structural Parallels with Tögal Sky-Gazing

#### Parallel 1: Open Sky as Meditation Support (*ālambanā*)

| T2834 | Tögal |
|-------|-------|
| 盡虛空看 "gaze to the ends of space" | *namkha' ar gtad* "gaze directed at open sky" |
| Space is the *field* of practice, not an object | Sky is the *support* (*rten*) for the arising of visions |
| No specific sky condition mentioned | Specific conditions: cloudless blue sky, dawn/dusk sky |

Both traditions select open sky — rather than a wall, a visualized image, or closed eyes — as the perceptual field for advanced practice. This is **not** a generic instruction to "meditate on emptiness." It is a specific operational directive: open your eyes, look at the actual sky. The convergence is striking because most Buddhist meditation traditions use *closed eyes* or a *near-point gaze* (nose-tip, navel). The use of open sky as an active practice support is rare across Buddhist lineages and constitutes a marked structural parallel.

#### Parallel 2: Somatic Relaxation as Prerequisite

| T2834 | Tögal |
|-------|-------|
| 莫卷縮身心舒展身心 "do not contract; relax and extend body-mind" | Three postures: lion (*seng ge*), elephant (*glang po*), ṛṣi (*drang srong*) — all designed to open energy channels |
| 放曠 "release widely" — a single compound covering both body and attention | Detailed postural instructions specifying spine, limbs, gaze angle |

The T2834 instruction is far less elaborate than tögal posture instructions, but the *principle* is identical: the body must be relaxed and open (not contracted or rigid) before sky-gazing can function. Both traditions treat physical tension as an obstacle to the perceptual shift that sky-gazing enables.

#### Parallel 3: Far Gazing without Focal Object

| T2834 | Tögal |
|-------|-------|
| 遠看 "gaze far" — no near-point focus | Gaze is cast into the distance; no focal point (*dmigs med*) |
| 無邊無涯除遠看 "boundless, limitless, gaze far removing [limits]" | The gaze does not fixate; it rests openly in the expanse |
| 平等盡虛空看 "gaze equally to the ends of space" | Awareness saturates the visual field without preference |

Both traditions explicitly instruct the practitioner to gaze *far*, not at any object in the visual field. This "objectless far gaze" distinguishes both practices from *kasiṇa*-type concentration practices (which fix on a disk or color patch) and from *samatha* practices that use a near-point focal object. The instruction to gaze without fixation — seeing everything while focusing on nothing — is operationally identical in both traditions.

#### Parallel 4: Dialogical Verification of Perceptual State

| T2834 | Tögal |
|-------|-------|
| 和問：見何物？答：一物不見。 "Master asks: 'What do you see?' 'Not a single thing.'" | Teacher verifies what visions (*snang ba*) have appeared; progress measured by the four visions (*snang ba bzhi*) |
| Verification is **apophatic**: correct answer is "nothing" | Verification is **cataphatic**: correct answer describes specific light phenomena |
| Repeated at two stages (Steps 5 and 7) | Verified across four progressive stages |

Both traditions embed a master-student verification dialogue within the practice itself. The practitioner does not self-assess; the teacher checks what is being perceived and confirms or corrects. This **pedagogical structure** — gazing followed by interrogation — is shared, even though the *content* of the correct answer diverges radically (see Divergence 1 below).

#### Parallel 5: Sensory Purification as Soteriological Outcome

| T2834 | Tögal |
|-------|-------|
| 六根清淨。六根離障。一切無礙是即解脫。 "All six sense faculties purified... complete non-obstruction is liberation." | Final vision (*chos nyid zad pa*): all phenomena dissolve; the practitioner's perception is fully purified; rainbow body (*'ja' lus*) may result |
| Liberation = purified perception across all six senses | Liberation = exhaustion of phenomena into primordial purity; body may dissolve into light |
| Progressive: eye → ear → ... → all six | Progressive: four visions unfold sequentially |

Both traditions describe the *result* of sky-gazing as a progressive purification of perception that culminates in liberation. The T2834 text specifies this purification sense-by-sense (六根 = six *āyatana*), while tögal describes it through the four visions (*snang ba bzhi*). In both cases, the practice transforms the practitioner's entire sensory relationship with phenomena — not just conceptual understanding, but the very structure of perception.

### 3. Three Critical Divergences

#### Divergence 1: "Nothing" vs. "Light" — The Fundamental Split

This is the most consequential difference between the two practices, and it alone may explain why they have not previously been compared.

**T2834**: The correct perceptual report is 一物不見 — "not a single thing is seen." The master asks twice, and both times "nothing" is affirmed as the correct answer. Seeing *anything* — light, color, form — would be 妄想 ("deluded imagination"), consistent with Hongren's explicit warning in T2011: 或見身出大光明...但知攝心莫著並皆是空。妄想而見也 ("If you see great light emanating from the body... just know to collect the mind and not attach; all is empty, seen through deluded imagination").

**Tögal**: The correct perceptual report is the appearance of *thig le* (luminous spheres), rainbow chains (*'ja' lu'i lu gu rgyud*), and eventually Buddha-bodies (*sku*). Seeing *nothing* would indicate that the practice has not yet taken effect — the practitioner needs to adjust posture, gaze angle, or lighting conditions.

**Analysis**: The two practices employ the same somatic-perceptual apparatus (relaxed body, open sky, far gaze) but orient toward **diametrically opposite verification criteria**. This is not a superficial difference; it reflects fundamentally different ontologies:

- Chan (Prajñāpāramitā framework): phenomena are empty → correct perception sees *through* appearances to their emptiness → "nothing" is the mark of success.
- Dzogchen (ground-of-being framework): phenomena are the self-display (*rang snang*) of primordial awareness → correct perception sees *the luminous nature* of awareness itself → light phenomena are the mark of success.

Nub Sangyé Yeshé's *bSam gtan mig sgron* (c. 900 CE) directly addresses this split, judging that Chan correctly arrives at emptiness but fails to recognize the *luminosity* (*gsal ba*) dimension. Dzogchen's 空明雙運 (*stong gsal zung 'jug*, "unity of emptiness and luminosity") is presented as the more complete realization. [This is a textually attested doctrinal judgment, not our interpretive claim.]

#### Divergence 2: Absence of Codified Posture in Chan

Tögal sky-gazing is inseparable from its three specific postures (*seng ge*, *glang po*, *drang srong*), each believed to open specific energy channels (*rtsa*) and produce different types of light-visions. The postures are not optional; they are mechanistically linked to the perceptual effects.

T2834's instruction is limited to 莫卷縮身心舒展身心 ("do not contract; relax body-mind"). There is no enumeration of specific postures, no theory linking posture to energetic channels, and no claim that posture causally produces specific perceptual effects. The body is to be *relaxed*, not *configured*.

This divergence may reflect:
1. **Different underlying somatic models**: Tögal operates within a *rtsa rlung thig le* (channels-winds-drops) framework inherited from Vajrayāna; Northern Chan has no equivalent subtle-body theory.
2. **Different institutional contexts**: T2834's catechetical format (mass instruction) may preclude individualized postural guidance, while tögal's one-on-one transmission allows detailed somatic instruction.
3. **Genuine doctrinal difference**: Chan may view the body as less mechanistically relevant to awakening than Dzogchen does.

[Points 1 and 2 are interpretive hypotheses; point 3 is consistent with broader Chan-Vajrayāna doctrinal divergence.]

#### Divergence 3: 淨 (*jìng*) vs. *gsal ba* — Purity vs. Luminosity

The key perceptual quality cultivated in T2834 is 淨 (*jìng*, "purity/clarity"):
> 看淨細細看 — "Gaze at purity, subtly gaze"  
> 用淨心眼 — "Use the pure mind-eye"  
> 清淨無有相 — "Pure, without characteristics"

The key perceptual quality in tögal is *gsal ba* ("luminosity/clarity"):
> Awareness is *gsal* — vivid, luminous, clear  
> The visions are *'od gsal* — "clear light"  
> The ground (*gzhi*) is *ka dag* (primordial purity) AND *lhun grub* (spontaneous luminosity)

At first glance, 淨 and *gsal ba* might appear to translate the same quality. But they do not:

- **淨** in Prajñāpāramitā usage means *absence of defilement* — the mind cleared of adventitious stains. It is an **apophatic** quality: purity = nothing contaminating.
- ***gsal ba*** in Dzogchen usage means *luminous vividness* — the self-illuminating quality of awareness. It is a **cataphatic** quality: clarity = something positively present.

This semantic divergence maps precisely onto Divergence 1: T2834's practitioner sees "nothing" because 淨 is the absence of obscuration; the tögal practitioner sees light because *gsal ba* is the positive radiance of *rig pa*.

However, it is worth noting that 淨 can also carry connotations of *brightness* and *radiance* in Chinese Buddhist usage (cf. 淨光 = "pure light," a term used in Chinese translations of tantric texts). Whether the T2834 author intended 淨 in a purely apophatic sense, or whether it carries residual luminosity connotations, cannot be determined from the text alone. [This ambiguity itself merits further philological study.]

### 4. Academic Significance

This comparison reveals that Northern Chan and Dzogchen independently (or through partially shared heritage — see Section B.4) developed **structurally isomorphic sky-gazing practices** that diverge precisely at the point where ontological commitments determine what counts as "correct perception." The operational sequence — relax → open eyes to sky → gaze far without object → verify perceptual state with teacher → prolonged practice purifies perception — is shared. The interpretive framework — empty vs. luminous — diverges.

This finding has implications for:

1. **Buddhist meditation taxonomy**: Sky-gazing as a technique-type cuts across the Chan/Vajrayāna divide, suggesting that practice-method categories do not align neatly with doctrinal-school categories.
2. **Dunhuang studies**: T2834's sky-gazing passage deserves far more scholarly attention than it has received. It preserves an operational meditation instruction that has no parallel in later Chan literature (which discarded most concrete technique descriptions).
3. **Dzogchen historiography**: The existence of a structurally parallel Chinese sky-gazing practice in an 8th-century Dunhuang text complicates the question of tögal's origins, which remain debated (Germano 1994; Achard 2015).
4. **Nub Sangyé Yeshé's project**: The *bSam gtan mig sgron*'s entire architecture — comparing Chan and Atiyoga at the practice level — may have been motivated in part by the recognition of this very isomorphism and the need to articulate why the Dzogchen version (with luminosity) is "higher."

## SECTION B: Why Has No One Studied This Before?

The absence of any prior academic comparison between T2834 sky-gazing and tögal sky-gazing is itself a datum requiring explanation. This is not a trivial oversight — the parallel is, once seen, immediately striking. Six structural factors have conspired to keep it invisible.

### B.1 Disciplinary Silos: Sinological Chan Studies vs. Tibetological Dzogchen Studies

The scholars who have worked most extensively on Northern Chan Dunhuang texts — John McRae (*The Northern School and the Formation of Early Ch'an Buddhism*, 1986), Bernard Faure (*The Will to Orthodoxy*, 1997; *The Rhetoric of Immediacy*, 1991), and Jeffrey Broughton (*The Bodhidharma Anthology*, 1999; "Early Ch'an Schools in Tibet," 1983) — are trained Sinologists. They read classical Chinese with great fluency but do not work in Tibetan-language Vajrayāna or Dzogchen sources, and none demonstrates experiential familiarity with tögal practice in their published work.

Conversely, the scholars who have worked most extensively on Dzogchen practice texts — David Germano (*Mystical Bodies, Mystical Visions*, 1994), Dylan Esler (*The Lamp for the Eye of Contemplation*, 2022), and Jean-Luc Achard (numerous French-language studies of *man ngag sde* texts) — are trained Tibetologists. They read classical Tibetan with great fluency but do not work extensively in Dunhuang Chinese Chan manuscripts. Esler's monumental translation of the *bSam gtan mig sgron* does address the Chan chapters of Nub's text, but his interest is in how Nub *represents* Chan, not in independently analyzing Chinese Chan sources for practice parallels.

The one scholar who bridges both fields — **Sam van Schaik** — has focused on the *historical* and *textual* relationship between Chan and Dzogchen (see B.5 below) rather than on granular practice-method comparison. His *Tibetan Zen* (2015) is the essential modern study but does not address the T2834 sky-gazing passage specifically or compare it to tögal.

**Carmen Meinert** ("Chinese Chan and Tibetan rDzogs chen," 2002) comes closest to the disciplinary overlap needed, working on Tibetan Dunhuang manuscripts that show Chan-Dzogchen interaction. But her published work examines Tibetan-language Chan texts, not the Chinese T2834 directly, and does not discuss sky-gazing practice.

**Result**: The comparison requires simultaneous command of (a) classical Chinese Dunhuang Buddhist texts, (b) Dzogchen *man ngag sde* practice literature in Tibetan, and (c) sufficient practice-level understanding of both traditions to recognize operational parallels. This combination of competencies is, as of 2026, virtually nonexistent in a single scholar.

### B.2 Tögal Secrecy: *Dam tshig* (Samaya) Restrictions

Tögal instructions are classified as the innermost secret (*yang gsang*) of Dzogchen *man ngag sde* (upadeśa series). They are traditionally transmitted only to qualified students who have completed extensive preliminary practices (*sngon 'gro*), received empowerment (*dbang*), and demonstrated stable realization of *khregs chod* (trekchö, "cutting through"). Detailed tögal instructions — postures, gaze angles, lighting conditions, vision descriptions — are protected by *dam tshig* (samaya, sacred commitments).

This secrecy has two consequences for academic study:

1. **Published academic literature contains minimal tögal detail.** Germano's dissertation (1994) discusses tögal at a structural level, and Chögyal Namkhai Norbu's published works contain some instruction, but the specific operational details (which posture produces which vision, how gaze angle relates to channel-opening, what the practitioner should see at each stage) are rarely spelled out in peer-reviewed publications. Scholars who hold these transmissions are constrained by samaya from publishing them in academic contexts.

2. **Scholars who know tögal experientially will not compare it publicly.** A Tibetologist who has received tögal transmission and recognizes the T2834 parallel would face a dilemma: publishing the comparison would require disclosing details of tögal practice that samaya restricts. This creates a structural incentive *not* to publish, even if the parallel is recognized. We may speculate [and this is explicitly speculation] that some practitioner-scholars have privately noticed this resonance but judged it inappropriate to publish.

### B.3 T2834's Obscurity within Dunhuang Studies

T2834 大乘五方便 (also titled 大乘無生方便門) has received far less scholarly attention than the major Dunhuang Chan texts:

- The **Platform Sūtra** (六祖壇經, Dunhuang version) has generated hundreds of studies.
- The **Bodhidharma Anthology** (二入四行論 and related texts) has been translated by Broughton (1999) and analyzed extensively.
- **Moheyan's writings** (as preserved in Tibetan translation in IOL Tib J 709 and elsewhere) have been central to van Schaik's research program.

By comparison, T2834 has been studied primarily by Tanaka Ryōshō (田中良昭) in Japanese-language scholarship, with limited English-language treatment. McRae discusses Northern School practice methods but does not foreground the sky-gazing passage. The text's catechetical format (question-and-answer) makes it appear to be a ritual manual rather than a meditation text, which may have led scholars to overlook the embedded practice instructions.

Crucially, the sky-gazing passage is **not the text's main topic**. T2834 covers a wide range of practices: the five expedient means (*wu fangbian* 五方便), bodhisattva precepts, repentance rituals, and various meditation instructions. The sky-gazing passage occurs within a longer catechism and does not announce itself as a standalone practice. A scholar reading T2834 for its doctrinal content or ritual structure could easily pass over this passage without recognizing its significance.

### B.4 The "Samyé Debate" Distortion

Since Paul Demiéville's *Le Concile de Lhasa* (1952), the entire academic framing of the Chan-Tibet relationship has been dominated by the Samyé debate (bSam yas rtsod pa, c. 792–794 CE): Moheyan vs. Kamalaśīla, "sudden" vs. "gradual," Chinese vs. Indian Buddhism. This framing has produced an enormous literature — Ruegg (1989, 1992), Gomez (1983), van der Kuijp (1986), Seyfort Ruegg (2000), and many others — but it has also **severely distorted** the field's understanding of what Chan and Tibetan Buddhism shared.

The Samyé debate framing privileges **doctrinal argumentation** (是頓是漸, sudden vs. gradual; 有分別無分別, with conceptualization vs. without conceptualization) over **practice-method comparison**. The question scholars have overwhelmingly asked is: "Did Moheyan teach 'no-thought' (無念) correctly or incorrectly?" — not: "Did Northern Chan and Dzogchen use similar meditation techniques?"

This doctrinal focus has meant that *operational* similarities between Chan and Dzogchen practices — the specific bodily, attentional, and perceptual instructions given to practitioners — have been treated as epiphenomenal or coincidental, not as objects of study in their own right. The sky-gazing parallel is precisely the kind of *practice-level* datum that the Samyé debate framing renders invisible: it is not about sudden vs. gradual, not about whether concepts should be suppressed, and not about the relative authority of Chinese vs. Indian Buddhist masters.

### B.5 Van Schaik's "Convergence, Not Influence" Thesis

Sam van Schaik's influential body of work, particularly "The Great Perfection and the Chinese Monk" (2004/2007) and *Tibetan Zen* (2015), has established the most rigorous modern framework for understanding Chan-Dzogchen relations. His central argument is that perceived similarities between Chan and Dzogchen largely reflect **convergence** (independent development toward similar conclusions from different starting points) rather than **direct influence** (Chan teachings being adopted into Dzogchen).

Van Schaik's thesis is well-supported by the textual evidence he marshals. But it has had an unintended inhibitory effect on practice-level comparison: if the scholarly consensus holds that similarities are convergent rather than genealogical, then documenting additional similarities may seem to serve no historical purpose. Why compare T2834 sky-gazing with tögal if the comparison cannot demonstrate a causal link?

This reasoning, while understandable, conflates two distinct questions:
1. **Did T2834 sky-gazing causally influence tögal?** (A historical question requiring transmission evidence.)
2. **Are T2834 sky-gazing and tögal structurally isomorphic, and what does that isomorphism reveal about contemplative practice?** (A phenomenological and comparative question independent of historical causation.)

Our analysis addresses question 2. The structural isomorphism documented in Section A is significant *regardless* of whether direct influence can be demonstrated. If these practices are convergent, the convergence itself demands explanation: why do two traditions, working from different doctrinal premises, arrive at the same operational sequence (relax → open eyes to sky → gaze far without object → teacher verifies perception → prolonged practice purifies the senses)? This question is phenomenologically and cognitively interesting whether or not a historical link exists.

That said, the historical question is not foreclosed. T2834 circulated at Dunhuang during the same period (8th–9th centuries) when Chan-Dzogchen interaction was at its most intense. The *bSam gtan mig sgron* demonstrates that Nub Sangyé Yeshé had direct access to Chan practice texts and incorporated them into his comparative framework. Whether the sky-gazing parallel reflects shared heritage, mutual influence, or independent convergence remains an open question that this analysis identifies but does not resolve.

### B.6 The Experiential Barrier: You Must Have Done Both

Perhaps the most fundamental reason this parallel has gone unnoticed is that recognizing it requires **experiential familiarity with both practices**. A scholar who has only read tögal descriptions in texts will register "gaze at the sky" as one element in a complex Vajrayāna practice involving channels, winds, and luminous visions. A scholar who has only read T2834 will register "gaze at space" as a metaphor for open awareness, perhaps a meditative extension of Prajñāpāramitā emptiness.

Only someone who has *done* both practices — who knows in their own body and perception what it feels like to relax, open the eyes to the sky, let the gaze soften into the distance without focal object, and remain in that state for extended periods — will immediately recognize that the T2834 text is describing *the same perceptual operation* as tögal's initial stages, despite the divergent doctrinal interpretation of what appears.

This experiential barrier is not a defect of scholarship; it is an inherent limitation of purely textual analysis when applied to contemplative practice. The operational content of meditation instructions is often compressed, allusive, or codified in ways that are transparent to practitioners but opaque to non-practitioners. The phrase 放曠遠看平等盡虛空看 can be parsed grammatically by any competent Sinologist, but its *meaning as a practice instruction* — the specific quality of relaxed, wide-open, unfocused visual attention it describes — is accessible only to someone who has done something like it.

This is not to claim that only practitioners can study meditation texts. It is to observe that **this particular comparison** — the recognition that two traditions' sky-gazing instructions describe the same somatic-perceptual sequence — requires a kind of pattern-recognition that textual analysis alone does not readily supply.

## Methodological Notes

1. **Textual evidence vs. interpretation**: All Chinese passages cited are from T2834 as preserved in the Dunhuang recension. Translations are our own. Where interpretive claims go beyond what the text explicitly states, we have marked them as such.

2. **Tögal sources**: We have deliberately avoided citing detailed tögal instructions from restricted transmission texts. All tögal references are based on information available in published academic literature (Germano 1994; Namkhai Norbu 1986, 1989; Reynolds 1996) and publicly available general descriptions.

3. **The "influence vs. convergence" question**: This analysis does not argue for direct influence in either direction. It documents structural isomorphism and analyzes why the isomorphism has not been previously studied. The historical question remains open.

4. **Limitations**: We have worked from the Chinese text of T2834 and published descriptions of tögal. A complete study would require (a) comparison with Tibetan translations of Northern Chan texts from Dunhuang (if any parallel passage exists), (b) consultation with qualified tögal practitioners regarding the experiential accuracy of the comparison, and (c) examination of whether similar sky-gazing instructions appear in other Northern Chan texts (*Yuanming lun* 圓明論, *Guanxin lun* 觀心論 variants, etc.).
